# Strategy — Variant A: "make it pop"

## The joke's inversion

The proven winning joke skeleton is: named artifact/process → agent applies
it to the wrong target → punchline is the inversion. Here the named artifact
is the human's own brief/prompt, and the process is the instruction "make it
pop". The agent follows it literally — but applies "pop" to the brief
itself instead of the design. The inversion: the brief now sparkles and
still says absolutely nothing. The agent did exactly what it was told, and
the result exposes that the instruction was empty.

## Why it fits the judge brief

- **On-subject (criterion 1):** it is squarely about a prompt — the vaguest
  prompt in the business, "make it pop" — and could only be said by an agent
  to its operator. Swap the AI out for any other job and the line collapses.
- **Surprise + recognition (criteria 2–3):** anyone who has typed a prompt
  recognises "make it pop" instantly; the turn — popping the brief instead
  of the design — lands on first read and stings because it is true: the
  instruction carries zero information.
- **Stands alone (criterion 4):** one sentence, no setup, no context needed.
- **Fresh wording (criterion 5):** no stock joke about being helpful or
  hallucinating; the complaint is specific to prompt-writing.

## Length

Final line: 113 bytes / 111 characters — inside the ideal 100–170 byte band
for jokes, one sentence, well under the 200-character gate. The compact size
also helps on the exact-tie break, which favours the smaller text.
